# ISO 9001 Document Control: Keeping Documented Information Under Control

ISO 9001 Document Control: Keeping Documented Information Under Control is the way an organization ensures that information used to operate its processes remains accurate, easy to find, suitable for its purpose, and protected against unintended use of incorrect versions.

## Current edition note

ISO 9001:2026, the sixth edition, was published on 16 September 2026 and replaces ISO 9001:2015 as the current edition. This article discusses general documented-information management practices and does not interpret individual clauses. Organizations should consult an official copy of ISO 9001:2026 and ask their own certification body about applicable transition arrangements.

## Why documented information needs control

Documents can support decisions, process execution, quality checks, and organizational learning. When the status of information is unclear, people may follow outdated instructions, miss requirements, or struggle to retrieve evidence.

Control does not mean every activity needs a lengthy procedure. Organizations should determine what information is necessary for effective process operation, taking into account their size, activity complexity, process interactions, risks, and people’s competence.

## Documents and records serve different purposes

Documented information may function as:

- **Information used to perform work:** instructions, procedures, specifications, blank forms, work standards, or checklists.
- **Records providing evidence:** completed forms, inspection results, reports, monitoring data, approval evidence, or activity records.

Understanding these functions helps determine how information is created, updated, used, stored, and retained.

## Six practical controls

### 1. Clear identification

Give information identifiers that help users recognize what they need. These may include a title, reference code, date, owner, status, and revision where appropriate. Use a consistent naming approach that remains easy to understand.

### 2. Review and approval

Ensure information is reviewed by appropriate people before use. Approval should indicate that its content has been assessed for its intended purpose. For documents affecting quality or safety, define reviewers with appropriate competence and authority.

### 3. Distribution, access, and use

Users should be able to locate relevant information when needed. Define storage locations, permissions, distribution methods, and how obsolete versions are withdrawn or marked to prevent unintended use. Not everyone needs permission to edit documents.

### 4. Change control

Changes should be traceable to an appropriate level. The organization may define who proposes, reviews, approves, and implements changes; what changed; and how affected users are informed. Controls should be proportionate to the document's purpose and risk.

### 5. Storage and protection

Keep information legible, retrievable, and protected from loss, damage, unauthorized changes, or disclosure. For digital information, consider backups, role-based access, device security, and recovery. For physical records, consider storage conditions and damage prevention.

### 6. Retention and disposition

Define how long records need to be kept and how they will be disposed of when retention ends. Consider process needs, contracts, laws, and other obligations. Avoid destroying evidence still required or retaining sensitive information without a reason.

## A simple example: product inspection instructions

Imagine an inspection team using product inspection instructions. The instructions should have clear identification and status, be approved before use, be available at the point of work, and be revised when the inspection method changes.

When a new revision is issued, the organization should make sure users understand the change and that the previous version is no longer used as the active instruction. Completed inspection forms, meanwhile, should be retained as evidence according to the defined retention rules.

This shows that document and record control are not merely administrative activities. They support consistent process execution and help the organization trace what happened.

## Common mistakes

- Keeping multiple files named “final,” “final revised,” or “latest final” without reliable status identification.
- Changing work instructions without updating the controlled reference.
- Sharing files without ensuring recipients use the current version.
- Creating so much documentation that workers cannot find useful information.
- Disposing of records without checking retention requirements and obligations.
- Assuming automatic backups are sufficient while access, recovery, integrity, and change control are overlooked.

## Initial self-check

Use these questions as a starting point, not as a formal audit checklist:

- Do users know where to find current documents?
- Can document status—such as draft, approved, or obsolete—be identified?
- Are changes reviewed and approved by appropriate people?
- Is unintended use of obsolete versions prevented?
- Are access and protection appropriate to information sensitivity?
- Can records be retrieved and remain legible for as long as needed?
- Are retention and disposition rules responsibly defined?

## Conclusion

Effective ISO 9001 document control is not about the number of procedures or folders. It is about ensuring the information people need is available, suitable, protected, and managed throughout its lifecycle. Start with critical processes, understand users’ needs, and apply simple, consistent controls.

Documented information management must be tailored to the applicable edition of the standard, organizational context, and relevant legal, contractual, and stakeholder requirements. This article is general guidance, not an official interpretation of the standard or a guarantee of audit or certification outcomes.
